Lines on the Land

Every state with more than one district, over its own ground, in grey so the politics show. Switch every map at once with the bar, or one state with its buttons. The number on each button is how many seats that map gives Democrats with 2020 votes; the chart under each state shows how 10,000 or more neutral maps split those seats, and where the other maps land.

Every map here is usable: each district is one piece on real Census borders and within 1% of equal population (all 1,272 stored maps checked).

Official: the map in use, as the toys approximate it from block-group centres. Fair Lines: the toys' own solver. Both were made usable on real borders: stray pieces join the neighbouring district they touch most, then block groups along district edges move one at a time, never splitting a district, until every district is within 1%.

ReCom: neutral maps drawn by computer without party data (Moon Duchin's group's method: merge two districts, split them along a random tree, 50,000 times or more per chain, two chains, populations within 1%). The map shown is the typical one: among the maps sampled, the one nearest the middle seat count with the most ordinary shape.

Counties, ZIP areas, Towns, Schools: the same ReCom, with links inside one county, ZIP-code area, town or school district made 20 times less likely to be cut, so those stay whole. Rivers & ridges: links that cross a big river, a lake, a 150 m ridge or a river-basin line are the ones it prefers to cut.

Ties (county) and Ties (ZIP): districts grown from Meta's Social Connectedness Index, keeping places whose people are friends with each other together, with a compactness limit; redrawn on real borders.

The chart: how many of the neutral maps (10,000 or more per state) give Democrats each number of seats, for the neutral set shown (counties whole when a non-neutral map is shown). Marks: O official, F Fair Lines, T social ties (county), Z social ties (ZIP). Not settled means that set's two chains disagreed on seat counts, so its chart is rougher than the others.

Sources: USGS 3D Elevation Program (relief), USGS National Atlas 1:1,000,000 (rivers, lakes), Census 2020 block groups and shared borders, the redistricting toys' vote estimates.
